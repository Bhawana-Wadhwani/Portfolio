Listening to chickens: acoustic stress detection on working farms

Birds tell you they're in trouble long before they look like it. We built a Raspberry Pi listening device to catch that signal, and cut mortality by 60% across participating farms.

The problem

On a commercial poultry farm, by the time you can see that something has gone wrong, you've already lost birds. Heat stress, ammonia buildup, water line failures, disease onset — these all announce themselves hours before they become visible as bird deaths, but the announcement is audible, not visual.

Farm operators know this instinctively. Experienced staff will tell you they can hear when a shed "sounds wrong." But that knowledge lives in a handful of people, doesn't scale across dozens of sheds, and doesn't work at 3am when nobody's listening.

The signal was already in the room. Nobody was in the room to hear it.

The research foundation

Before building anything, we grounded the approach in existing work on avian stress vocalisation. There's an established body of research showing that poultry — chickens and turkeys in particular — produce measurably distinct calls under stress conditions, differing in frequency, duration, and rate from baseline flock sound.

Reference paper

Stress Detection and Classification of Laying Hens by Sound Analysis

Lee, Noh, Jang, Park, Chung & Chang — Asian-Australasian Journal of Animal Sciences, 2015

Read the paper

120 Hyline brown layers in a temperature-controlled chamber in Jinju, South Korea. Roughly 657 hand-labelled samples, classified with three hierarchical SVMs.

This mattered for two reasons. First, it meant we weren't guessing at whether the signal existed — the literature said it did. Second, it gave us a starting point for what to actually listen for, rather than throwing raw audio at a model and hoping.

Two numbers from the study shaped how we built. The authors started with 54 acoustic features and cut them to 8 through correlation-based selection, still reaching 96.2% detection accuracy — evidence that this problem doesn't need a large model, it needs the right features. And their false positive rate was 9.6%: entirely respectable in a paper, and completely unworkable on a farm.

Where the paper stopped and we started

The study ran on 120 caged hens in a temperature-controlled chamber, with stress induced deliberately and every sample labelled by hand. A working shed has twenty thousand birds, ventilation fans at 70–80 dB, feed lines, and nobody available to annotate anything. The paper proved the signal exists. It didn't prove you could hear it over the fans, and that gap was the actual project.

The device

We built a test rig deliberately cheap and deliberately boring. Anything that had to be babied wasn't going to survive a working poultry shed.

Four microphones feed a single Pi per shed; audio is buffered locally, pushed to AWS, and flagged calls trigger an operator alert.

Compute
Raspberry Pi, one per shed
Input
Four-microphone array, positioned for full floor coverage
Local storage
SD card buffer, so a dropped connection doesn't mean lost data
Processing
AWS — audio analysis and stress call detection
Output
Alert to the operator responsible for that shed

Why four microphones

A single microphone tells you something is happening. Four tell you where. Distress is rarely evenly distributed across a shed — it clusters around the failure. A water line problem at one end sounds different from a ventilation issue in the middle, and knowing which end to walk to is most of the value.

Why the SD card mattered more than it should have

Rural connectivity is unreliable. An architecture that assumed a live connection would have silently dropped exactly the data we most needed — you lose signal during a storm, and a storm is precisely when heat and ventilation stress spike. Buffering locally and syncing when possible meant the gaps closed themselves.

The part that was actually hard

The technical build wasn't the difficult part. The difficult part was that a poultry shed is extremely loud, and almost all of that noise is normal. Thousands of birds, fans running, feed lines moving. Baseline is not silence — baseline is a wall of sound.

Which meant the real work was in defining what "normal" sounded like for a given shed, at a given bird age, at a given time of day — and then detecting deviation from that, rather than detecting stress calls in isolation.

Design constraint

An alert that fires when nothing is wrong is worse than no alert at all. Operators stop trusting it within a week, and then the system is dead regardless of how good the underlying detection is.

This is why the reference study's 9.6% false positive rate — perfectly fine in a paper — was a number to beat rather than a target to hit.

This shaped the threshold decisions heavily. We tuned conservatively toward fewer, higher-confidence alerts, accepting that we'd miss some events in exchange for the alerts we did send being worth walking across a farm for.

Working with operators, not around them

The tool only worked because the people using it already had the instinct it was augmenting. Farm staff didn't need to be convinced that sound carries information — they needed a system that watched the sheds when they couldn't.

That framing changed the product. It stopped being "AI that monitors your farm" and became "a second pair of ears on the night shift" — which is both more honest about what it did and far easier to trust.

Result

Rolled out state-wide across the farm network, the system contributed to a 60% reduction in bird mortality across participating farms. Beyond the mortality number, the operational shift was that problems got caught in the window where they were still cheap to fix.

What I'd do differently

Spend longer on the per-shed baselining period before going live. We front-loaded rollout speed and paid for it in early alert noise that we then had to tune out shed by shed.
